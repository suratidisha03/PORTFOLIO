Which merchandising decisions actually move sales?

Academic research · Retail shopper experiment

216-person experiment · additive products drove the significant gains · +2.1% projected sales lift · +1.0% projected unit growth.

Role: Designed the experimental conditions, ran the ANOVA analysis, and built the merchandising recommendation.

01 / Marketing Question

Which merchandising decisions actually move sales in a detergent category reset?

02 / Context

Retailers reset categories often, but not every shelf change earns its cost. This project modeled a Walmart detergent category reset, testing whether specific merchandising decisions (eye-level placement, brand blocking, and cross-sell adjacency for additive products) produced a statistically meaningful sales difference, rather than assuming any reset would help.

03 / Data & Inputs

A 216-person shopper experiment testing multiple merchandising conditions, including eye-level product placement, brand blocking between competing detergent brands, and cross-sell placement of additive products next to base detergent.

04 / Approach

Used SPSS to run an ANOVA across the tested merchandising conditions, comparing simulated sales and unit outcomes between treatment groups to determine which changes produced a statistically significant difference and which didn't.

The lift didn't come from where we expected.

Repositioning the base detergent by itself wasn't the driver. The statistically significant gain came from placing additive products in cross-sell adjacency next to the base product: shoppers who noticed the pairing were more likely to add the complementary item, not just switch which detergent they picked up.

06 / What That Meant for Marketing

Recommended prioritizing cross-sell adjacency for additive SKUs over further base-product shelf optimization, since that was the change the data actually supported.

07 / Recommendation & Impact

A projected sales lift of +2.1% and a projected unit growth of +1.0% from the recommended cross-sell configuration. These are projected outcomes from the experimental model, not an observed retail result.

09 / What the Data Said

Across the 216-person experiment, the significant gain came from cross-sell adjacency for additive products, not from repositioning the base detergent. The projected outcomes, a +2.1% sales lift and +1.0% unit growth, both trace back to that one condition: the obvious lever (moving the hero product) wasn't the one the data supported.

10 / Reflection

If this moved into a live pilot, test the cross-sell placement in a small set of stores before rolling it out chain-wide, to confirm the projected lift holds outside the experimental model.
