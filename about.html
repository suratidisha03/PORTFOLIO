About

From graphic design to marketing analytics.

I started as a graphic designer. That's where I learned to actually look at things. When I moved into marketing, I brought that eye with me.

I think the best marketers are the ones who can't turn off the part of their brain that notices everything. Why did that post travel? Why did this email get opened and that one didn't? What made someone stop on that ad for three seconds longer than the last one? I've been asking those questions for years, long before I had a job title that said I should.

When I moved into marketing, I brought that eye with me. I'm not the person who briefs the designer and waits. I build the thing, then I measure it, then I rebuild it better.

That curiosity is what eventually led me to marketing analytics, where creativity and data come together. The real challenge was never making something visually appealing. It was understanding why people stop scrolling, remember a brand, or choose one product over another. Today, I'm most excited by work that blends both.

I care about work that actually does something, not just looks good in a deck. I'm in Chicago, actively looking, and I genuinely love talking to people who care about this stuff the same way I do.

What I do

How I actually think about the work

I like understanding how the pieces connect. A campaign, a caption, an email subject line, a search result, a follow-up message after someone signs up: they can look like separate jobs, but they're usually the same story, told in different rooms. I've never been able to look at just one of them at a time.

That's probably the graphic designer in me. I still think in terms of what someone sees first, what they notice next, and what makes them stay. But somewhere along the way I got just as interested in what happens after, whether the idea I liked in the room actually worked once it met real people.

I like bringing structure to creative ideas so they can go somewhere, not just live in a deck. And numbers, to me, were never really about performance. A number is just someone's behavior, written down. It tells you whether the thing you made landed the way you meant it to.

Marketing, for me, has always started with understanding people first. Everything else is just the form that understanding takes.

Currently exploring

What happens after someone clicks

Most of my work so far has lived in the moment someone first notices something: a post, an ad, a subject line. Lately I'm more curious about what happens next. Someone clicks, signs up, buys once. Do they come back? What makes them trust a brand enough to stick around, instead of responding once and disappearing?

That curiosity is pulling me toward customer journeys and lifecycle marketing, HubSpot, marketing automation, the slower architecture of a relationship instead of the moment of first contact. It's also pulling me toward customer experience more broadly, and toward AI-assisted workflows I want to use not to move faster for its own sake, but to spend more of my time on the parts that actually need a person paying attention.

I don't think of this as a list of things I'm learning. It's closer to a question I keep returning to: how does a brand earn someone's trust slowly, and keep it?
